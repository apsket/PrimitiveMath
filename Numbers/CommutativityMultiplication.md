# On the Commutativity of Multiplication of Natural Numbers

That the natural numbers commute under multiplication is one of the earliest properties that people learn since young age. The usual ways for its justification involve experimentation using different pairs of numbers, references to the geometric interpretation as areas or organizing objects in a grid format where visual acceptance of the property becomes obvious under such circumstances. Personally, I always felt these justifications were enough to convince myself of the truth of the commutativity, but yet remained somewhat incomplete into providing further insight of the property itself.

In early math, multiplication is introduced as a repeated addition such as $5\times3$ being taken to mean that one must add $5$ to itself $3$ times, i.e. $5\times3 = 5 + 5 +5$. This particular way of introducing multiplication is the most natural as it builds on the previously mastered concept of addition, but it leaves properties such as commutativity obscure. In more advanced math, multiplication of natural numbers is defined either recursively when treating numbers as ordinals or through the Cartesian product. While the latter is as a concrete approach where commutativity discloses itself naturally without recurring to "limiting" mental models, I think there is something to gain from continuing to construct multiplication through addition, in particular to highlight the symmetry of the operation and bridge the concrete with the abstract.

**Set-Theoretic Approach to Multiplication of Natural Numbers**

Define mutiplication $n\times m$ to measure the cardinality (i.e. count the number of elements) of the union of $m$ disjoint sets $V_i$ of size $n$. This definition reflects the repeated addition definition of multiplication, but making a reference to something concrete and intelligible such as sets. What does it mean for the sets $V_i$ to have the same cardinality? Having the same number of elements means we can always find a bijection between any two of these sets $V_i, V_j$. In simpler terms, you can pair elements of $V_i$ with elements in $V_j$ such that all elements in each set are included in exactly one pairing. Now consider extending one such bijection between $V_i, V_j$ to consider all remaining sets $V_k$. Now all elements in the union of these sets have been considered and can be identified by two labels: the original set $V_i$ to which they belonged and the multi-matching across sets to which they were assigned.

How many elements are in total after the union? We know from the definition that there are $n\times m$ elements, but we could also re-count based on the multi-matching across sets. We know multi-matchings don't share any elements and each contains exactly $m$ elements. We also know there are $n$ such multi-matchings because there is a distinct one for each element in a given set. These multi-matchings could be used to create new sets that are disjoint and exhaustive over all original elements. Counting based on these multi-matchings, one can see that there are in total $m\times n$ elements, the union of $n$ disjoint multi-matchings of size $m$. Thus, we have $n\times m = m\times n$.

We can also highlight other views stemming from this definition and insight. The total count of elements as defined by the operational union of sets would be $n+n+...+n$ where the summand $n$ appears exactly $m$ times, one for each set $V_i$. After the matching across sets, the count can be re-framed as $m+m+...+m$ where the summand $m$ appears exactly $n$ times.
